# Tool: Considerations for Reliance (Audit Report Writing Toolkit)

URL: https://www.theiia.org/globalassets/site/auditing-report-writing-toolkit.pdf
Author: Paula Michaels, for The Institute of Internal Auditors (IIA)

This tool references standards from the International Standards for the Professional Practice of Internal Auditing. Internal auditors may also consult the IIA Practice Guide, "Audit Reports: Communicating Assurance Engagement Results," from which most of this paper was sourced.

## Understanding the stakeholders / users of internal audit reports

Internal audit reports should consider the diversity of report recipients and their individual information needs. Recipients vary based on organizational structure, audit type, and related recommendations. The Chief Audit Executive (CAE) should establish report-distribution guidelines with the board and senior management (CEO, CFO, legal counsel, etc.). Internal audit reports are typically distributed to: process owners and management; senior management; the board; and other stakeholders as deemed necessary (such as external auditors).

Per Standard 2440 - Disseminating Results: "The chief audit executive must communicate results to the appropriate parties." A standard distribution list often exists and is adjusted per engagement. Recipients may include managers with direct responsibility for the audited activity and individuals with authority to act on recommendations. The CAE (or designee) may coordinate with management on the distribution list; final issuance and distribution decisions remain the CAE's responsibility.

Internal audit reports may be considered confidential and distributed on a need-to-know basis. Condensed versions may be created for sharing with other entities/units in the organization. Different formats may be created depending on the recipient: for example, it may be sufficient to provide board members with an executive summary rather than the entire detailed report.

Depending on industry (such as the public sector), reports may be publicly available. Per Standard 2440.A2, before releasing results to parties outside the organization (if not otherwise mandated by legal/statutory/regulatory requirements), the CAE must: assess the potential risk to the organization; consult with senior management and/or legal counsel as appropriate; and control dissemination by restricting use of the results.

## Report content and structure

The style and format of written internal audit reports varies across organizations. Report structure could be consistent with the organization's communication templates and practices, reflecting organizational culture and/or incorporating suggestions from senior management and the board.

Per Standard 2420 - Quality of Communications: "Communications must be accurate, objective, clear, concise, constructive, complete, and timely." The content and level of detail should be determined by the needs of the audience. It may be appropriate to use different formats/versions customized for the audience. Questions to consider about the audience(s):

- Who are the most important readers of the report?
- How much do they know about the audited activity?
- How do they plan to use the report?
- How do the identified issues impact the reader?

A typical report may include these components:

- Audit report title
- Objective (purpose of engagement)
- Scope (audited activities, nature and extent of work, scope limitations)
- Background (brief synopsis of the activity being audited or an explanation of the process)
- Recognition (positive aspects of the area/activity audited, or appreciation of cooperation)
- Engagement rating (ranking, outcome, e.g. satisfactory, marginal, unsatisfactory, pass, fail)
- Conclusions (summary opinion/assessment of the engagement, often highlighting critical observations)
- Observations (also called findings), each listed in order of significance (grouped by activity if applicable) and possibly including:
  - A title and reference
  - Criticality rating (measure of risk significance: high, medium, low, critical, significant)
  - Statement of facts (condition, criteria, cause, effect/risk), substantiated with relevant examples, data, analytics, tables, or charts
  - Audit recommendations (corrective action to mitigate the identified risk)
  - Management's action plans (corrective action, activity owner, target date for completion)
- Distribution list

The use of Standard 2430 - "Use of 'Conducted in Conformance with the International Standards for the Professional Practice of Internal Auditing'" may be indicated only if supported by the results of internal audit's quality assurance and improvement program.

## Report issuance

Engagement results should be communicated per the agreed communication plan. Timely communication allows management to take appropriate corrective action. Timing depends on several factors:

- Audit engagement type: for regular engagements in the annual internal audit plan, the CAE sets the issuance timetable per defined policies and procedures. Good practice is to issue the draft report within a few days of the exit meeting and the final written report within two weeks of the draft. For special engagements requested outside the annual plan to address an urgent issue, issuance ordinarily takes priority over regular engagements, to effectively and efficiently address higher-risk situations. For complex, lengthy engagements covering multiple audited activities (branches or units with many departments), interim reports or status updates could be issued upon completion of each audited activity for immediate consideration of observations and corrective action.
- Interim communication: for high-risk observations, it is prudent and typical for the CAE to verbally discuss items well in advance of the formal written report, and to authorize an interim report to management so action plans can be implemented immediately, prior to issuance of the final report. ## The executive summary

The stakeholders' desire to continue reading a report often depends on the impression of the executive summary, which should provide a clear and concise overview of the engagement results and efficiently deliver critical information with a persuasive, well-substantiated key message to readers.

The executive summary generally highlights good practices observed during the audit and any significant steps management has taken to improve governance, risk management, and internal controls. It should not contain technical jargon or internal audit methodologies; such information belongs in the detailed report if needed for a more in-depth understanding. Key components of an executive summary generally include:

- Introduction, objectives, scope, and engagement results
- Conclusions for the audited activity/processes
- A summary of significant observations or key messages
- Concerns encountered with management relating to establishing corrective actions, deadlines, and/or situations where the CAE concludes that management has accepted a level of risk that may be unacceptable to the organization

### Introduction and scope of the internal audit engagement

The introduction provides basic information about the entity, activity, or process audited. The scope may indicate the period covered, the type of internal audit conducted (assurance engagement, advisory/consulting engagement, or follow-up audit), specific risks, relevant systems, and/or the departments or functions covered.

Per Standard 2410.A1: "Final communication of engagement results must include applicable conclusions, as well as applicable recommendations and/or action plans. Where appropriate, the internal auditors' opinion should be provided. An opinion must take into account the expectations of senior management, the board, and other stakeholders and must be supported by sufficient, reliable, relevant, and useful information." The internal auditors' conclusion on the condition of the audited activity/process helps the reader understand the significance of the observations. Financial impact caused by internal control weaknesses and irregularities can be used to convey the significance of observations.

### Summary of significant observations

The executive summary generally contains significant observations or key messages from the report and may include concerns encountered with management relating to establishing corrective actions. It is often beneficial to include a dashboard listing findings in table form, depicting the number of observations/recommendations per audited activity, according to their importance.

Key observations can be summarized in a positive manner (focusing on enhancement) or a negative manner (focusing on weaknesses). Internal audit is encouraged to acknowledge satisfactory performance when applicable and to show the trend (positive or negative) compared to prior audits of the same activity. The executive summary may include repeat observations from a previous audit, and information on action plans from previous audits that have not been completed or whose implementation deadlines have expired. In such cases it is beneficial to include historical information on repeat observations and management's action plans.

## Typical contents of an audit report

Most written audit reports contain variations of these elements:

- Executive summary: usually a one-page report succinctly describing the audit from engagement plan to outcomes
- Objectives: specific objectives of the engagement, related to the engagement client's objectives
- Scope: scope of the engagement in relation to the objectives; scope and objectives should align with relevant International Professional Practices Framework Standards
- Background: information pertinent to the activity under review
- Conclusion: a summary of engagement outcomes and a conclusion on the audit objectives
- Internal audit opinion: an audit opinion on the risk and control environment of the process under review
- Management action plan: a detailed action plan developed by management to address the engagement observations; this ("Details of the Review") can be repeated as often as required in a report

Note: this IIA toolkit is written for internal-audit engagement reports (financial/operational/compliance audits within an organization), not for website/technical audits specifically. Its dual-audience framing (full report vs. executive-summary-only distribution to different stakeholder tiers) and its "condensed version per recipient" convention are the parts most directly transferable outside the internal-audit context; the ISO/Standard citations (2420, 2430, 2440, 2410.A1) are internal-audit-profession-specific and do not apply verbatim to a website audit deliverable.
